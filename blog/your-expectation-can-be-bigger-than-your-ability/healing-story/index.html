When the Doctor Has Said There Is Nothing More They Can Do

Your Expectation Can Be Bigger Than Your Ability

← Read the main teaching
This is the real-life story approach to the teaching Your Expectation Can Be Bigger Than Your Ability.

The consultant was still talking, but somewhere along the way Daniel stopped hearing him.

He could see his lips moving. He could hear fragments of sentences.

“...we have tried...”

“...the disease has progressed...”

“...at this stage...”

Then came the sentence that seemed to gather everything else into itself.

“There isn't much more we can do.”

Daniel looked at his wife.

She was sitting beside him with both hands wrapped around her handbag. She had not said anything for several minutes. Her eyes were wet, but she was fighting the tears.

Daniel looked away.

He didn't want to cry there.

Not in front of the doctor.

Not in front of his wife.

He was the one who was supposed to be telling everybody that things would be all right.

On the drive home, neither of them spoke much.

His wife reached across several times and held his hand.

That night, after everyone had gone to bed, Daniel sat alone in the living room.

The house looked exactly as it had looked the day before.

There were children's shoes near the door. A cup someone had forgotten was still on the dining table. His phone was charging beside him. Somewhere down the hallway, one of his children coughed and turned in bed.

Everything looked normal.

But nothing felt normal anymore.

For the first time, Daniel found himself looking around the house and wondering what life there would look like without him.

Who would take care of them?

Would he see his children grow up?

Was this how his story was going to end?

And then came the thought he was almost ashamed to admit:

God, I don't want to die.

He had prayed many impressive prayers since the diagnosis.

That wasn't one of them.

There were no carefully chosen words this time.

No attempt to sound strong.

Just a frightened man sitting before God.

“Father, I want to live.”

And perhaps that is where faith sometimes begins—not with a person pretending not to be afraid, but with someone bringing the fear itself into the presence of God.

Daniel knew what the doctors had said.

He had seen the scans.

He had watched his body change.

He knew the pain was real.

He knew the doctors were not trying to destroy his faith. They had treated him, explained things to him and done what their training enabled them to do.

But sitting there that night, another thought came to him.

The doctors had told him what they could no longer do.

They had not told him what God could no longer do.

There is a difference.

And sometimes, when you are frightened, that difference can become very difficult to see.

Human beings naturally build expectations from what is available to them.

If the treatment is working, hope rises.

If the scan improves, hope rises.

If the doctor sounds optimistic, hope rises.

And when those things disappear, expectation can disappear with them.

Without realizing it, the treatment can become more than treatment.

The doctor can become more than a doctor.

The medical report can become more than a report.

They can become the final authority on what we are permitted to expect.

That night Daniel opened his Bible.

He wasn't searching for a formula that would force God to heal him.

He wasn't looking for a verse he could repeat enough times to make the diagnosis disappear.

He needed to remember who God was.

He read the accounts of Jesus and the sick.

People came to Christ with conditions they could not solve themselves. Some had exhausted the help available to them. Others were carrying situations that seemed beyond ordinary human intervention.

And they still came.

That mattered to Daniel.

Because perhaps faith was not asking him to pretend the diagnosis wasn't real.

Perhaps faith was asking him something much deeper:

Who gets the final word over your expectation—the limit of human ability, or the ability of God?

Daniel closed his Bible.

The cancer was still there.

Tomorrow's appointment was still on the calendar.

Nothing visible in the room had changed.

But Daniel prayed differently.

“Father, the doctors have told me what they can do. I am grateful for them. I will receive the help they can give me.

“But they are not You.

“I don't know what tomorrow will look like. I don't know how You will answer me. But I know that their limitation is not Your limitation.

“So I am asking You to heal me.”

And he did.

Not once.

Again and again.

When he was courageous, he prayed.

When he was frightened, he prayed.

When he felt strong, he prayed.

When he could barely find words, sometimes all he could say was:

“Father, I still trust You.”

And he continued receiving medical care.

There was no contradiction in that.

Taking medication did not mean he had stopped trusting God. Going for another scan did not mean his faith had failed. Asking the doctors questions did not make him less spiritual.

Medicine was something available to him, so he received it gratefully.

But he would no longer confuse the means through which help could come with the God in whom his confidence ultimately rested.

That is what it means to say that God is our Source.

And this matters far beyond sickness.

Sometimes a salary disappears, and because the salary was the only way we could imagine provision coming, we begin to believe provision itself has disappeared.

Sometimes someone refuses to help us, and because that person was the only route we could see, we assume there is no other route.

Sometimes an opportunity closes, and we interpret the closed opportunity as though God Himself has said, “There is nothing more I can do.”

But a channel can reach its limit without God reaching His.

A human being can say no without exhausting God's possibilities.

A plan can fail without exhausting God's wisdom.

And a doctor can reach the boundary of medicine without reaching the boundary of God.

That doesn't mean we know beforehand exactly what God will do in every situation. Nor does faith give us permission to turn our desires into promises God has never made.

It means something simpler and stronger:

We must never use the size of human ability to measure the size of God's ability.

So perhaps you are facing something right now that has made you quietly reduce your expectation.

You have done the calculations.

You have considered your age.

Your money.

Your qualifications.

Your connections.

Your diagnosis.

Your past failures.

The opportunities available to you.

And somewhere inside yourself you have concluded:

This is probably as far as I can go.

Maybe it is as far as you can go.

That is not necessarily the same thing.

You can acknowledge the facts without worshipping them.

You can listen to wise people without making them God.

You can receive every legitimate help available to you without making that help your source.

You can do responsibly what is within your hands and still trust God concerning what is completely outside them.

That is not denial.

That is participation.

And perhaps tonight your prayer does not need to be complicated either.

Perhaps it can simply be:

“Father, this is bigger than me.

I cannot make this happen.

But my inability is not Your inability.

Show me what is mine to do, and help me trust You with what only You can do.”

Because sometimes we reach the end of ourselves and mistakenly think we have reached the end of possibility.

We haven't.

Your ability has an end.

God's does not.
