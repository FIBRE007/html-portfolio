Stop Trying to Earn Grace

Understanding What It Means to Work From Grace, Not for Grace

By Fadoju Tosin

← All articles

Many sincere Christians know that salvation is by grace, yet without realizing it, they can begin to live as though God's continued goodness must be earned.

When things are going well, we may conclude that we have prayed enough, obeyed enough, served enough, or lived well enough to deserve God's favour. When things go wrong—or when we fail—we may immediately wonder what we did wrong to make God withdraw His goodness.

This is where we must understand the difference between grace and performance.

Grace is God's goodness freely extended to us, originating in who He is rather than in our ability to deserve it. Performance, as used here, does not simply mean working hard or doing good. It means making what we do the basis upon which we believe we qualify for God's acceptance, goodness, or help.

The problem, therefore, is not prayer, obedience, discipline, giving, service, or hard work. These are important parts of Christian living. The problem begins when we make them the reason God should be good to us.

Paul makes the distinction unmistakable:

“And if by grace, then it is no more of works; otherwise grace is no more grace.”Romans 11:6 (WEB)

Grace begins where earning ends.

When performance becomes our confidence before God, something happens inside us: our failures begin to feel like condemnation.

If good performance makes me believe I qualify for God's goodness, then poor performance will naturally make me believe I have disqualified myself. But the gospel gives us another foundation:

“There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus…”Romans 8:1 (WEB)

This does not make failure unimportant. Grace does not teach us to excuse sin, ignore mistakes, or avoid responsibility. Rather, grace gives us a secure place from which to repent, learn, change, and continue walking with God without allowing failure to become our identity.

That is why trying to earn grace turns every failure into condemnation.

And this leads to another important truth: grace earned is no longer grace. If God's goodness were simply wages for our spiritual performance, it would be payment rather than grace. Our confidence, therefore, must ultimately rest somewhere other than ourselves. Paul describes believers as those who “rejoice in Christ Jesus, and have no confidence in the flesh” (Philippians 3:3, WEB).

This is also where genuine rest becomes possible.

A person constantly trying to qualify never knows when enough is enough. Have I prayed enough? Have I served enough? Have I been good enough? There is always another performance to measure.

But Hebrews speaks of entering God's rest:

“For he who has entered into his rest has himself also rested from his works, as God did from his.”Hebrews 4:10 (WEB)

This rest is not passivity. Working from grace does not mean that we stop working; it changes why and from where we work.

Paul himself demonstrates this beautifully:

“But by the grace of God I am what I am… I worked more than all of them; yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me.”1 Corinthians 15:10 (WEB)

Notice the order: grace first, then labour.

Grace did not make Paul inactive. Grace became the foundation of his activity. He was not working to make grace available; grace was already at work in him, and he responded through faithful participation.

Conclusion

This is the invitation of grace: stop working to deserve what God gives by grace, and begin working because His grace has already met you.

Pray. Obey. Serve. Give. Learn. Work diligently. Take responsibility. When you fail, repent and rise again. But do not turn these things into a price you must pay to persuade God to be gracious.

Grace gives us a different foundation for living. We can expect mercy and help in time of need (Hebrews 4:16), wisdom when we ask (James 1:5), strength in weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9), sufficiency for every good work (2 Corinthians 9:8), and God's supply for our needs (Philippians 4:19).

These provisions do not make us passive; they give us confidence to participate. We pray expecting God's help, work expecting His supply, ask expecting His wisdom, and face failure without surrendering to condemnation.

Let Christ, rather than your performance, become your confidence.

Grace is not the reward waiting at the end of your performance. Grace is the foundation from which your Christian life begins.

You can work from what you have received, rather than working to become worthy of receiving it.
